Why cross-border businesses are reviewing operations

In October 2026, the Canadian Federation of Independent Business reported that 47% of surveyed small businesses said profits had decreased since the August escalation in the Canada–U.S. trade dispute. Its report also found that one-third of respondents reported a high impact from U.S. tariffs (30%) or Canadian counter-tariffs (32%). These are survey findings from Canadian small businesses and should not be treated as a measure of every company on either side of the border.

In the United States, the Federal Reserve Banks' 2026 Report on Employer Firms identified cost increases and tariff-related expenses as important financial challenges for many surveyed employer firms. Together, these sources show why businesses are reviewing pricing, supplier exposure, customer communication and the day-to-day work required to keep operations running.

  • Businesses need current product, price and availability information across channels.
  • Customers may ask more questions about shipping, delivery windows and product availability.
  • Teams need clear ownership for order exceptions and supplier-related updates.
  • Owners need visibility into routine work without spending the day chasing status updates.

What companies can control

A company cannot control government policy, exchange rates, freight markets or another business's supplier decisions. It can control the quality of its internal records, the clarity of customer communication, the speed of routine follow-up and the way work is assigned.

1. Maintain accurate product and inventory information

Keep product descriptions, variants, images, availability and pricing aligned with approved business records. Establish a clear process for changes and a second check for high-impact updates.

2. Make customer communication consistent

Prepare approved templates for order confirmation, dispatch updates, delays, backorders and common product questions. Do not promise a delivery date or product availability unless the business has verified it.

3. Create an exception-handling workflow

Define which issues a support assistant can resolve, which require an operations manager and which need a supplier, carrier, legal or customs professional. Record the owner and next action for each unresolved case.

4. Build operational continuity around documented tasks

Store process instructions, task ownership and escalation contacts in a shared system. This reduces dependency on one employee remembering every step and makes coverage easier during busy periods.

5. Separate administrative support from regulated decisions

Remote assistants can organize data and routine communication. Customs classification, trade compliance, tax, legal advice, employment decisions and financial approvals must remain with appropriately qualified and authorized professionals.

Build a practical 30-day operations reset

Week 1: identify the top three recurring delays and document the current workflow. Week 2: create checklists and approved customer response templates. Week 3: test delegation on one defined task group and review accuracy daily. Week 4: measure response times, unresolved exceptions and rework, then decide what to improve next. This is an operational improvement plan, not a guarantee of financial results.
