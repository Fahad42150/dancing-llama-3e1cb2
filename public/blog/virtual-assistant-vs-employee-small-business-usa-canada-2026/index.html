Why small businesses in the USA and Canada are rethinking how they staff work

Many owners are facing the same operational question: how can the business keep serving customers, managing daily tasks and growing sales without adding fixed costs faster than revenue? Recent research points to pressure from inflation and input costs, difficulty finding qualified staff, trade-policy uncertainty and the challenge of making new technology work with existing processes.

For some companies, the answer is a new employee. For others, it may be outsourcing a defined process, using a virtual assistant (VA), improving automation or combining these approaches. The right choice depends on the work, the required control, data sensitivity, workload consistency and the real total cost—not simply the hourly rate.

1. What the latest 2026 research says about business pressure

United States: Gusto's November 12, 2026 State of Small Business report found that 59% of surveyed owners said input-price changes negatively affected their business over the prior 12 months. It also reported that 67% of small businesses use AI for at least some tasks. Among owners who struggled to hire, skills gaps and the affordability of qualified labour were important barriers.

Canada: Statistics Canada's third-quarter 2026 business conditions report identified inflation (41.6%), recruiting skilled employees (25.2%) and input costs (24.1%) among the leading obstacles businesses expected to face. The same report found that 25.2% expected difficulty recruiting skilled employees and 15.5% expected difficulty retaining them.

Cross-border uncertainty: The Canadian Federation of Independent Business reported in October 2026 that 47% of surveyed businesses said profits had fallen since the escalation of the Canada–U.S. trade dispute in August. Trade and policy changes do not affect every company equally, but uncertainty can make owners cautious about permanent costs and investment.

These findings do not prove that outsourcing is always cheaper or better. They do explain why owners are reviewing staffing models, administrative workload and productivity.

3. Which business tasks are suitable for outsourcing?

Outsourcing tends to work best when tasks are repeatable, measurable, documented and do not require the assistant to make high-risk decisions without approval. Examples include:

  • Administrative support: inbox organization, calendar coordination, data entry, document formatting and spreadsheet updates.
  • Customer service: first-line email and chat responses, FAQ handling, order-status updates and escalation of complaints.
  • eCommerce operations: product uploads, descriptions, image organization, variant checks, inventory updates and routine store maintenance.
  • Lead and sales administration: CRM updates, lead research, follow-up reminders and preparation of reports.
  • Marketing assistance: content scheduling, basic research, formatting and campaign administration with owner approval.
  • Operations coordination: task tracking, supplier follow-ups, order documentation and recurring status reports.

Keep financial approvals, legal decisions, hiring and firing, sensitive HR matters, access to payment credentials and final customer-policy decisions with authorized personnel unless a properly governed specialist arrangement is in place.

4. The hidden cost of doing everything yourself

Owners often calculate the cost of outsourcing but overlook the cost of fragmented attention. When the owner spends several hours each week answering routine enquiries, updating listings, chasing documents and entering the same information into multiple systems, that time is unavailable for sales, service design, cash-flow planning and leadership.

Before outsourcing, measure the current process for two weeks:

  1. List recurring tasks and how often each occurs.
  2. Estimate time spent, delays, rework and errors.
  3. Separate tasks that require owner judgement from tasks that follow a checklist.
  4. Define what a successful result looks like: response time, accuracy, completion rate or backlog reduction.
  5. Compare the full cost of an employee, a contractor, an agency and process automation—including training and oversight.

5. AI is not a complete replacement for people or process

Businesses across North America are adopting AI, but tool adoption alone does not guarantee productivity. Ricoh's October 21, 2026 U.S.–Canada workplace technology survey found that disconnected workplace tools can create added complexity, security and governance challenges. The Bank of Canada's September discussion of economic adaptation also noted that many businesses are using AI for routine tasks while broader process transformation takes time.

A practical model is human-led, technology-assisted operations: use approved tools for drafting, sorting and summarizing; require human checks for accuracy, customer commitments and sensitive decisions; and document who is accountable when an automated workflow fails.

6. How to outsource safely in the United States and Canada

  • Write a scope of work with specific tasks, hours, response times and deliverables.
  • Use confidentiality and data-processing terms appropriate to the information handled.
  • Give each worker an individual account with least-privilege access; enable multifactor authentication.
  • Never share owner passwords, payment card credentials or unrestricted administrator access unnecessarily.
  • Set approval limits and escalation rules for refunds, discounts, customer complaints and financial actions.
  • Start with a small paid pilot, review quality and expand only after the workflow is stable.
  • Review applicable privacy, tax, employment and contractor-classification rules in the relevant jurisdiction.
